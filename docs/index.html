The problem

The trap in sizing charging infrastructure

UoW Bikes charges a mixed fleet of shared e-bikes and e-cargo bikes at a depot behind a constrained 15 kW grid connection — a bigger import limit means a costly, slow DNO reinforcement. Demand swings with term-time, weather and multi-year growth. Size the hub for the average and it strands bikes on busy days; size it for the worst case and capital sits idle.

😬 Naive design — sized for average demand

5 kWp PV · 0 kWh battery · 4 bays

£47,179 /yr worst case

Cheapest on a normal day — but in the high-demand future its unmet-charging penalty balloons and it strands 1 bike in 21 (95.3% service).

🧱 Oversized design — reliable but wasteful

25 kWp PV · 50 kWh battery · 20 bays

£78,000 capital

Nearly 3× the capital of the robust hub for the same 99.6% guaranteed service — and a worse worst-case cost (£23,714/yr). The extra battery adds cost, degradation and embodied materials, not resilience.

🏆 Robust design — balanced, reliable, cost-aware

15 kWp PV · 0 kWh battery · 8 smart-managed bays

£19,232 /yr worst case

−59% worst-case cost and guaranteed service lifted to 99.6% — a small expected-cost premium buys protection against the demand tail.

💡 The key insight: smart charging is the cheapest robustness lever

A bike returned in the evening only needs to be ready by morning, so the controller schedules charging into sunny and cheap off-peak hours. That flattens the load below the grid limit — so at this connected site a battery does not pay. Storage only enters the design when the grid connection weakens to ≈9 kW or below. Method

Real data → 8,760-hour physics → five decision rules → stress-tested

In plain terms: the model chooses the design that still performs well when the future is worse than expected. Everything is reproducible with one command (python run_analysis.py) and re-verified by CI on every push.

UoW e-bike demand→Coventry solar (PVGIS)→West Midlands carbon (ESO)→8,760-hour simulation→150 designs→5 decision rules→robust recommendation
1

Real demand, real solar, real carbon

Demand is calibrated to the UoW Bikes use case using open shared-micromobility evidence (DfT monitoring data, 2022–24); the pipeline auto-ingests the official UoW Bikes CSV when present. Solar comes from the PVGIS API for Coventry; grid carbon from National Grid ESO (West Midlands).

2

15 probability-weighted futures

Low/Medium/High usage × five growth paths, evaluated at the 5-year design horizon — the demand range is observed, not assumed, and each future gets a likelihood.

3

Hourly energy balance

For all 8,760 hours: PV serves demand first, surplus charges the battery then exports; deficits draw battery then the capped grid; what's left is unmet — the failure robust design must avoid.

4

150 designs × 5 decision rules

Every PV × battery × bay combination scored under naive, stochastic, CVaR, minimax-regret and maximin rules — the robust pick must beat the worst case, not the average.

5

Stress-test the conclusion itself

500-sample Monte-Carlo, Sobol global sensitivity, and a penalty × grid × horizon sweep: the recommendation wins in 100% of 35 combinations — it is not an artefact of one assumption.

6

Validate & operate

9/9 outputs and inputs sit inside published benchmark ranges; an LP dispatch verifies the greedy controller; a MATLAB/Simulink digital twin shows how the chosen hub runs day-to-day.

Battery threshold

When does a battery actually pay?

At the campus hub's 15 kW connection, the recommendation is 0 kWh of battery — smart charging alone keeps the load under the limit. Weaken the connection to around 8–10 kW and storage starts entering the optimal design. This is not an anti-battery result: it avoids unnecessary battery cost, degradation and material impact when smart charging can solve the problem — and it tells you exactly when storage becomes worth it.

🔋 Battery sustainability & circularity

  • Storage is conditional, not default: the model adds a battery only when the grid is weak enough to need one — around 8–10 kW here.
  • Avoided material impact: skipping an unnecessary 20–50 kWh pack avoids its lithium, iron-phosphate and manufacturing footprint entirely.
  • LFP chemistry preferred where storage is needed — cobalt- and nickel-free, long cycle life, safer thermal behaviour.
  • Second life & recycling: weak-grid deployments should plan second-life reuse and end-of-life recycling from day one.
  • Degradation is priced in: the cost model ages the battery by cycling and calendar time, so replacements are never hidden.

“The most sustainable battery is sometimes the one you do not need to install.”

Theme 2 · How the batteries are used

Personal vs shared e-bikes — the physics of a day

A physics route model (rolling + gradient + aerodynamic force, drivetrain efficiency, motor-assist share) gives the energy cost of every kind of ride. The duty cycles then split: a personal bike takes one deep overnight home charge; a shared bike drains through many trips and tops up at the depot — cycling ~1.3× harder per year. That is why shared fleets need the managed hub.

Scale · Safety · Cost

Built to be copied — safely, and on budget

This is a decision-support prototype — it tells an operator what to build and when storage pays; detailed electrical design and installation remain a contractor task.

📈

Scalability

  • Site-agnostic by design: swap the PVGIS coordinates, the demand CSV and the grid cap — the same pipeline re-sizes a hub anywhere.
  • Campus → city: one hub serves ~360 bikes; a city fleet replicates hubs rather than up-sizing one (modular £29k units, no DNO reinforcement).
  • UK ↔ India transfer: under the British Council partnership the same model resizes for Indian solar yields (~1.6× Coventry) and 2/3-wheeler fleets — where the weak-grid storage result becomes the binding case.
  • Growth-proof: designs are sized across 0–15%/yr growth with S-curve saturation, so scale-up is already priced in.
🛡️

Safety

  • Chemistry first: where storage is used (weak-grid sites) we specify LFP — no cobalt/nickel, higher thermal-runaway onset than NMC.
  • Standards-mapped: BS EN 62485-5 (stationary batteries), BS EN 50604-1 (light-EV packs), BS 7671 + IP65-rated outdoor bays, Type-B RCD per charge circuit.
  • Fire & layout: battery cabinet outdoors with 1 m clearance and thermal cut-off; bays under a canopy — charging e-bike packs sheltered, ventilated and away from building escape routes (PAS 7061/7062 handling).
  • Electrical headroom: smart charging caps peak draw below the 15 kW connection — the controller enforces the limit in software and the supply is fused for it in hardware.
💷

Cost analysis

Where the £29,400 CAPEX goes

  • £19,232/yr worst-case total (annualised capital + O&M + grid energy + demand charges), LCOE £0.253/kWh delivered.
  • −£27,947/yr vs naive in the worst future — the robustness premium pays for itself in the first bad year.
  • Time-of-use tariff + £80/kW·yr demand charge modelled; PV residual value credited; battery replacement DoD- and calendar-aware.

Feasible with off-the-shelf kit, today

Mono-Si PV (20% module efficiency), standard 3 kW AC bays, optional LFP cabinet — no exotic hardware, no planning-triggering structures. The Simulink digital twin (matlab/chargingHubTwin.slx) reproduces the EMS behaviour for operations hand-over.

Carbon & energy impact

  • 3.7 tCO₂/yr avoided (marginal-grid basis; 55 t over the PV lifetime) — stated honestly on a consequential basis, not inflated.
  • 15% of charging served directly by on-site sun at the recommended size — every extra kWp raised self-consumption until export caps bind.
  • Displaces ~10,200 kWh/yr of marginal gas generation.
